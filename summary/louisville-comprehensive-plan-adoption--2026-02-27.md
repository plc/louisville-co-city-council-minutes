## Summary

The Louisville Planning Commission unanimously voted to recommend adoption of the city's comprehensive plan update after a 2+ year development process with extensive community involvement. The plan will guide Louisville's development for the next 10-15 years, focusing on maintaining neighborhood character while enabling targeted growth in select areas, with heightened emphasis on equity, inclusivity, and sustainability.

The central controversy centered on housing capacity projections. The plan projects a theoretical maximum of 4,000-5,000 residential units over 10-20 years, while the city's housing needs assessment identifies 2,500 units as the baseline if Louisville maintains its current percentage of Boulder County's population. Staff emphasized that the 4-5K figure is a high-level planning tool for infrastructure capacity, not a growth target, and actual development will likely be significantly lower due to market realities, existing development constraints, and continued public approval processes for major projects.

A critical legal constraint shapes the plan: Colorado state law explicitly prohibits municipalities from imposing growth caps in comprehensive plans or other regulatory documents. This means Louisville cannot legally set a hard ceiling on residential development, making the capacity range a planning estimate rather than a binding limit. Staff noted that roughly 3,600 of the projected units are on vacant land (most likely to develop), while the remainder are on already-developed properties unlikely to redevelop within the plan horizon.

Public comment reflected community divisions, with concerns about preserving small-town character balanced against desires to remain accessible to diverse residents and younger families. Commissioners acknowledged these tensions but emphasized that the comprehensive plan represents a framework for evaluation rather than prescriptive mandates, with all major development subject to future public hearings and policy balancing across multiple city goals.

## Key Points

- **Unanimous approval:** Planning Commission voted 5-0 to recommend adoption to City Council
- **Development timeline:** 2+ years of work with exceptional community participation
- **Housing capacity:** 4,000-5,000 unit theoretical maximum (planning tool, not growth target)
  - 3,600 units on vacant land; remainder on developed properties unlikely to redevelop
  - Expected actual development significantly lower due to market factors and development constraints
- **Housing needs baseline:** 2,500 units represents maintaining Louisville's current share of Boulder County population
- **State law constraint:** Colorado explicitly prohibits growth caps in comprehensive plans
- **Core plan elements:**
  - Preserve established neighborhood character and historic downtown/Oldtown
  - Enable targeted growth in select areas for economic development
  - Increased focus on equity, inclusivity, and community resiliency
  - Enhanced sustainability policies
- **Continued oversight:** All multifamily and large-scale projects require public hearings before Planning Commission and City Council
- **Commissioner requests:** More transparency in Appendix D methodology showing line-by-line capacity calculations

## Takeaways

This represents a major milestone in Louisville's long-term planning, attempting to balance competing community values: preserving small-town character while remaining accessible and fiscally sustainable. The distinction between "capacity" (what's theoretically possible) and "need" (baseline growth projection) proved central to public debate, with staff emphasizing the former is a conservative planning tool rather than a development goal. The state law prohibition on growth caps constrains Louisville's policy options, making process controls (public hearings, policy balancing) the primary mechanisms for managing growth rather than numerical limits. The plan's fate now moves to City Council for final adoption, with implementation spanning 10-15 years through incremental decisions shaped by both this framework and future public input.
