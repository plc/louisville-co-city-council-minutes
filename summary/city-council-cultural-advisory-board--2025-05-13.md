## Summary
The Cultural Advisory Board reported a transformative 2024 after transitioning from voting to advisory role, producing 27 events (20,000+ attendees) compared to previous years' limited activity. Chair J.R. Kettlesson credited the structural change with enabling greater efficiency, while public comment from Friends of Louisville Arts, Sciences & Humanities (Flash) highlighted Colorado's paradox—#1 in arts participation, among worst in government funding—and proposed a new inclusive performing arts space.

## Key Points
- **2024 record year:** 27 events, 20,000+ attendees (most ever)
- **Role transition impact:** Advisory structure (vs. voting/quorum for $500+ expenses) dramatically increased efficiency
- **Public art:** 3 installations (pollinator mural, Marshall Fire pet memorial, community resilience sculpture)
- **Steinbaugh mural delayed:** Structural issues; funding available for 2025
- **Concerts in Park:** 5 concerts in 2024 (750-1,200 each), reduced to 4 in 2025 due to rising costs
- **Art grants:** 5 awards ($2k each), 15-20 applications, new EDI scoring matrix
- **New traditions:** Block party (4th year, June 8), Movie on Main (economic vitality tie-in)
- **EDI programming:** "Bingo's Birthday" (children), "Transformative Stories" (trans community)
- **Flash advocacy:** Leah Franks proposed inclusive performing arts space (meetings started with Center Stage, Northline Arts, Create Boulder)
- **Budget concerns:** Federal arts grants canceled (millions), SCFD uncertainty, cultural services budget cuts
- **Colorado context:** #1 state in arts participation, among worst in government funding

## Takeaways
The CAB's governance shift from quasi-legislative (voting on every $500+ expense) to purely advisory unlocked operational nimbleness—27 events versus historical scarcity. Chair Kettlesson's admission he was "skeptical at first" but now sees the efficiency gains validates council's 2024 board restructuring across all advisory bodies. The old voting requirement created bureaucratic friction (quorum, meetings for routine contracts); the new model trusts staff execution with board strategic input.

Flash's Leah Franks articulated a tension Louisville hasn't resolved: Colorado leads the nation in arts participation but municipalities lag in funding support, creating over-reliance on SCFD (Scientific and Cultural Facilities District) sales tax. With federal NEA/NEH grants evaporating and economic uncertainty threatening SCFD revenue, Louisville's cultural infrastructure faces compounding risks. The proposed inclusive performing arts space addresses a genuine gap—Steinbaugh Pavilion lacks accessibility and year-round functionality—but Flash's multi-stakeholder convening (Center Stage, Northline, Create Boulder) suggests an answer may require regional collaboration beyond Louisville's budget alone.

The concerts-in-park reduction (5 to 4) due to rising talent costs exemplifies cultural programming's vulnerability to inflation without proportional budget increases. The new EDI scoring matrix for grants (quantifying "what does a 1-5 mean for equity?") represents Louisville's attempt to operationalize abstract values, though examples like "Transformative Stories" (trans narratives) may test political headwinds in current climate.
