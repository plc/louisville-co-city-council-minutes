# Summary

Louisville City Council and Revitalization Commission (LRC) held a joint meeting to address governance friction in their working relationship. The LRC's attorney characterized Louisville's intergovernmental agreement (IGA) as "highly unusual" compared to the 15 other Colorado urban renewal authorities he represents, describing it as "more of an oversight document than a collaboration document." Key constraints include a unique three-step approval process for redevelopment agreements (LRC → council → LRC again, taking up to three months) and council budgetary approval beyond the statutory requirements for separate governmental entities. The LRC requested more autonomy to operate within approved guardrails, noting they work closely with staff, have an assigned attorney, and include a council member. The meeting focused on identifying "wonky" process inefficiencies ahead of major Front & Center and underpass projects, with the TIF funding mechanism expiring in 2032.

## Key Points

- **First Collaborative Meeting:** Joint session to address "awkward moments" in council-LRC relationship, particularly approval back-and-forth processes
- **Attorney Assessment:** LRC's IGA provisions "unique" compared to 15 other Colorado URAs; current structure adds oversight beyond statutory requirements for separate governmental entities
- **Three-Step Problem:** Redevelopment agreements require LRC approval → council approval → LRC final approval (3-month process); "highly unusual" per attorney since LRC has statutory authority for agreements consistent with urban renewal plan
- **Budget Double-Dip:** Council approves LRC budget before AND after LRC adoption; budget amendments follow same pattern (LRC April → council May → LRC final)
- **2023 IGA Amendment:** Allowed LRC to approve grants within budgeted amounts (facade/property improvement) without council re-approval
- **Risk Assessment:** Redevelopment agreements using net new TIF revenue pose minimal risk (only property owner at risk if TIF not generated)
- **Staff Safeguards:** Staff reviews all LRC applications before commission sees them, vetting for program alignment and blight mitigation factors
- **"Goes Rogue" Concern:** Attorney noted he's seen URAs "go rogue" in 25-30 years but mechanisms exist for council intervention; "if URA isn't implementing council policy, you have a much more fundamental structural problem"
- **TABOR Exemption:** LRC not subject to TABOR since it doesn't impose new taxes (collects TIF from existing mill levies); may be origin of oversight concern
- **Timeline Pressure:** TIF funding expires 2032 but organization continues in perpetuity; major Front & Center and underpass projects upcoming
- **Comparison to Other Boards:** Facade improvement program operates with autonomy within approved parameters; LRC seeks similar trust-based model

## Takeaways

The attorney's framing—Louisville's IGA as "oversight document" versus "collaboration document"—exposed a governance philosophy disconnect. Urban renewal authorities exist precisely to empower subject-matter experts to implement council's adopted plan without re-litigating every agreement. Louisville's three-step redevelopment approval process suggests institutional distrust despite layered safeguards: staff review, attorney assignment, council member participation, and statutory authority limits. The "goes rogue" specter haunts the room, but the attorney's response is telling—in three decades, he's rarely seen it, and when it happens, the problem isn't procedural friction but fundamental misalignment between council policy and URA execution.

The LRC's request isn't radical autonomy; it's operational efficiency within approved guardrails. The comparison to the facade improvement program is apt: once council authorizes a program with parameters, commissioners shouldn't need council re-approval for every grant. The upcoming Front & Center and underpass projects will test whether Louisville's current IGA can accommodate complex, time-sensitive real estate development or whether the three-month approval cycle will strangle momentum. With TIF expiring in 2032, the window for demonstrable downtown revitalization success is shrinking—yet the governance structure optimizes for caution over velocity.
