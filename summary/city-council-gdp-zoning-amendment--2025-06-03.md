## Summary
Council advanced Ordinance 1896 (first reading) allowing General Development Plans in Planned Community zones to establish custom height/setback/lot coverage standards without strict "general conformance" to underlying zoning. Initiated by PorterCare Adventist Health Systems but broadly applicable, the amendment aims to provide more predictable master planning process aligned with council's business-friendly development review goals. Planning Commission recommended approval.

## Key Points
- **Ordinance 1896:** GDP zoning text amendment (first reading)
- **Initiated by:** PorterCare Adventist Health Systems (applies broadly to all PC-zoned properties)
- **Current rule:** GDPs must show "general conformance" to underlying zone districts (interpreted strictly since 2010)
- **Proposed change:** Custom yard/bulk standards allowed if consistent with comp plan and desired district character
- **Flexibility:** Still requires council approval; applicants must justify proposals
- **Properties affected:** Planned Community zones (30+ acres, single ownership, master planned)
- **Planning Commission:** Recommended approval (May 8)
- **Work plan fit:** Supports "business-friendly" development review process improvements
- **Question raised:** How to evaluate hospital standards when Louisville has only one hospital

## Takeaways
PorterCare's text amendment request reveals a tension in Louisville's 1983 Planned Community zoning code—the original intent was "contemporary land planning principles" and "custom zoning," but post-2010 staff interpretation locked GDPs into strict conformance with underlying zoning, defeating the purpose of master-planned flexibility. The current process forces developers to wait until PUD (Planned Unit Development) stage to request waivers, increasing uncertainty and investment risk.

The proposed fix shifts flexibility earlier in the process (GDP vs. PUD) while preserving council discretion. Staff's emphasis on "business-friendly" and "predictable" signals alignment with economic vitality priorities, but the text amendment's broad applicability (all PC zones, not just PorterCare) raises questions about unintended consequences. What prevents a developer from proposing wildly out-of-scale standards and arguing they're "contemporary planning"?

The safeguard is that council still approves each GDP, but the amendment removes the objective "general conformance" benchmark, replacing it with subjective "consistent with desired character" language. Council member Fahey's hospital question illustrates the challenge—without peer comparisons in Louisville, how do you evaluate what's appropriate? This puts more analytical burden on applicants (good) but also more decision-making burden on council (potentially problematic without clear criteria).
