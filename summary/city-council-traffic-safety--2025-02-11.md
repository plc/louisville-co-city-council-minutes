# Summary

Louisville City Council received a comprehensive presentation on the city's traffic calming strategy and Vision Zero initiative from engineering staff. The presentation covered existing infrastructure (bulb-outs, hawk signals, RFBs, raised crosswalks), evaluation methodology (the "three E's" of education, enforcement, and engineering), and results from the Via Appia road diet project, which successfully reduced speeds by 3-5 mph in most locations without reducing traffic volumes. Staff demonstrated commitment to accessibility through blind user experience testing at South Boulder Road, though mixed community reactions to Via Appia changes highlighted ongoing tensions between safety improvements and evacuation concerns from Marshall Fire survivors.

## Key Points

- **Vision Zero Framework:** Louisville adopts FHWA's safe system approach aimed at eliminating traffic fatalities and serious injuries through redundant safety measures
- **85th Percentile Metric:** Speed at which 85% of drivers travel; used as industry standard for gauging actual driving behavior vs. posted limits
- **Existing Infrastructure:** Extensive network of bulb-outs, hawk signals, rectangular rapid flashing beacons (RFBs), speed cushions, raised crosswalks mapped citywide
- **Three E's Evaluation:** Education (driver training, youth cyclist awareness), Enforcement (speed compliance), Engineering (proven countermeasures from FHWA)
- **Via Appia Results:** Road diet reduced speeds 3-5 mph at two of three measurement locations without impacting traffic volumes; mixed community reactions ("genius" vs. continued complaints)
- **Accessibility Focus:** Staff blindfolded testing revealed hostile driver behavior toward blind pedestrians; new ADA guidelines require audible push buttons separated by 10 feet
- **Upcoming Projects:** Raised crosswalk near elementary school (pending bid alternate approval); Madison Avenue traffic calming (50+ signatures after bike lane installation created perceived speeding)
- **Competing Interests:** Fire survivors want two lanes maintained for evacuation; Lafayette Street location saw no speed reduction despite road diet

## Takeaways

The presentation revealed a tension between data-driven traffic engineering and community perception. Via Appia's measurable success (3-5 mph speed reduction, maintained traffic flow) contrasts sharply with persistent resident complaints in Council Member Hearn's ward, particularly near Lafayette Street where the blind curve didn't achieve predicted speed reductions. The project's mixed reception—from "genius" praise to ongoing frustration—illustrates how initial skepticism can evolve but also how localized problem areas can undermine broader project acceptance.

Staff's blind user experience testing stands out as exceptional community engagement, directly experiencing hostile driver behavior (honking, profanity) while navigating Highway 42 crossings. This embodied approach to accessibility planning suggests a shift from compliance-driven ADA retrofits toward genuine understanding of vulnerable user experiences. However, the low from staff's 2024 reflections—Via Appia's contentious rollout—indicates that even well-intentioned, data-supported projects can exact significant emotional costs when community engagement doesn't fully prepare stakeholders for change.
