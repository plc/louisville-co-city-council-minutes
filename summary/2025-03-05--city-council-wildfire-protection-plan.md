# Summary

Louisville City Council and Fire Protection District Board held their first joint meeting since 2016-2017 to review the Boulder County Community Wildfire Protection Plan (CWPP). Boulder County Wildfire Partners presented the county-level CWPP as a "funnel" framework providing broad strategies for local adaptation, not neighborhood-granular mandates. Meg Halford acknowledged risk assessment limitations—vegetation-weighted fuel models show eastern Louisville as "low/moderate" risk despite Marshall Fire consequences—and emphasized the need for community-scale mitigation ("1, 2, 3, 5, even 10 homes out of 300 is not going to do anything"). Public comment criticized the city's previous wildfire risk assessment for excluding neighborhoods and failing to implement emergency operations recommendations, while praising fire district partnership.

## Key Points

- **First Joint Meeting Since 2017:** Fire Chief Wilson initiated meeting with city manager after noting years-long council-board disconnect
- **CWPP as Planning Framework:** County-level document provides broad strategies, not prescriptive mandates; serves as umbrella for local "mini CWPPs" and grant eligibility criteria
- **Risk Assessment Flaw:** Vegetation-weighted fuel models don't capture urban density/structure risk; eastern Louisville shows "low/moderate" despite Marshall Fire (probability low, consequences high)
- **"Fuel Model H":** Homes as fuel create urban conflagrations through home-to-home ignition; modelers working to integrate structure density into risk scenarios
- **Louisville Progress:** 220 acres mitigated 2023-2024, ~300 more planned; open space owns 2,000 acres balancing wildfire mitigation with wildlife/recreation objectives
- **Fire District Focus:** Expanding wildfire risk assessment citywide as "mini CWPP," don't need to reinvent wheel from county plan
- **Community-Scale Action:** Individual home hardening insufficient; requires neighborhood-level coordination especially on open space frontlines
- **National Framework:** Three buckets from 2009 Cohesive Fire Strategy: fire-adapted communities (preparedness/education), resilient landscapes (mitigation), safe/effective response (alert/evacuation)
- **Development Process:** 15 core team members + 100+ stakeholders (fire districts, scientists, fuel modelers) + public input from community events
- **Story Map Resource:** Simplified online version of 500-page document showing risk maps, treatment areas (2023 data), management recommendations
- **Public Critique:** Previous city manager dismissed resident concerns about county coordination, traffic management, home hardening; emergency ops assessment recommendations unimplemented

## Takeaways

The county CWPP's "funnel" model offers Louisville a pragmatic path: adopt broad strategies as local policy without duplicating the 500-page planning process. However, the risk assessment's structural flaw—vegetation bias that renders eastern Louisville "low/moderate" risk—creates a dangerous optics problem. Residents who survived the Marshall Fire see data visualizations that minimize their lived reality, even as Meg Halford acknowledges "fuel model H" (homes as fuel) isn't adequately captured. The disconnect between technical models and community experience risks undermining mitigation buy-in precisely where consequence (if not probability) is highest.

Tanya's public comment exposed governance failures beyond technical modeling: the previous city manager's documented dismissal of resident input, refusal to coordinate with county disaster management, and shelving of emergency operations recommendations. Her framing of fire district leadership as "my chief" versus city officials signals a trust asymmetry—residents view the fire district as responsive partners and the city as bureaucratically resistant. Chief Wilson's observation that boards haven't met since 2017 suggests institutional siloing that post-Marshall Fire urgency has finally forced open. The challenge isn't just expanding the risk assessment citywide (which staff plans to do) but rebuilding resident confidence that city leadership takes preparedness as seriously as the fire district does.
