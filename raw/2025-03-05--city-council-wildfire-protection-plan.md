# Louisville City Council Special Meeting: Boulder County Wildfire Protection Plan
March 5, 2025

## Introduction: First Joint Meeting in Years

>> Chief Wilson, Louisville Fire Protection District: "I met with city manager Fox about a month ago and I brought it to her attention that my board and the city council has not met for many many years like back probably 2016 2017 somewhere around that time. So, I suggested that we probably should have just a meet and greet and just kind of tell a little bit about the fire district to the city elected officials. It was suggested that we should probably bring in the CWPP and I said yes absolutely."

>> Mayor: "What we're going to focus on tonight is policy, where we are where we're going and get educated and we're going to take public comment. We're not going to be talking about individuals except perhaps as our efforts are organized. This isn't an effort to do that. It's to focus on the policies that we need to have in the ones we have in place, the ones we don't have in place, the ones we should have in place."

## Boulder County CWPP Overview

### Purpose & Scope

>> Meg Halford, Boulder County Wildfire Partners: "I want you to kind of think of it as kind of a funnel. The county is at that top level. And that top level just only means that it covers the entire county. It's not granular. It doesn't go down to the neighborhood level. And then you have the agencies, that's something like the fire protection district planning or even a town planning. And then when you go down to the communities, those are like your HOAs and that is where they are really focusing on their community within their boundaries itself."

>> Meg Halford: "The plan identifies risks across landscape, not neighborhoods. It identifies broad strategies to protect lives, homes and critical infrastructure. It is not prescriptive. All CWPPs at any level are not prescriptive. It's a living document for planning and implementation."

>> Meg Halford: "I've been dealing with CWPPs for over 25 years and our joke used to be they're a dusty document. But I think now with climate change and population growth and the size of fires, we're pretty determined or I'm pretty determined to make sure that this document is implemented. Is it perfect? No. Will we have changes? Yes. Will we adapt? Yes. And that's what this is. It adapts to changes and events."

>> Meg Halford: "It's not a regulatory or a legal document. It has no mandates in it. We do describe and discuss how important land use or wildfire building codes are in there but it is it is a planning document."

>> Meg Halford: "Really what's most important when you think of that funnel again is it really should link to that local level. How can we support how can this large planning document with broad recommendations then be used by local levels to come together as a collaborative effort. Not just the fire protection district but get the fire protection district, your local police, your civic leaders, your homeowners, your open space, your land managers at that table to discuss, okay, how can we now take this to our level and develop a guiding document."

### Grant Eligibility

>> Meg Halford: "It really helps to meet the criteria of state, federal, and local grant programs. In all grants, in most all grants, it asks are these projects or goals and objectives identified in a CWPP, a community wildfire protection plan or like document. So, it's really important because that's how if you're looking for funding is that yes, this meets this goal or these objectives for the city of Louisville or for Boulder County and that's how we seek funding."

>> Meg Halford: "Particularly important is funding is a question. We have local funding and we have state funding but federal funding right now is in question."

### Risk Assessment Limitations

>> Meg Halford: "I wanted to bring up wildfire risk. The CWPP as it's being developed, we do a risk assessment. It is very broad. And I wanted to put the two maps up there and kind of explain how this is interpreted and understand where maybe there are some things that need to be improved."

>> Meg Halford: "Right now the fuel models, the fire models, the vegetation is heavily weighted. And so that's why you're seeing that red on that top map. That's the risk potential because that's a lot of vegetation. As you transition into the eastern the urban areas that does not capture that. We know that. We know that this is an issue."

>> Meg Halford: "Folks who live here in the east go, 'Wow, that says low or moderate. Well, we just had the Marshall fire. Why aren't we high risk?' But understand that low or moderate risk is still a risk. It's still very important to understand that that doesn't mean you're not at risk and that you should not do anything. That doesn't mean that you shouldn't be preparing and doing the things that you need to do."

>> Meg Halford: "Risk equals probability times consequence. If you look at the low risk or moderate, and this is being respectful for the folks who went through the Marshall fire, the probability of a fire that magnitude happening in the east, it's low. It happened. But the consequences were very high. You're more likely to start a wildland fire in the West. Lightning, recreation, campfires. So just understand we recognize and you all should recognize that that is still an important risk."

>> Meg Halford: "Until we can get those fuel modeling and that's at other higher levels to build in a different model and other indices such as homes density, etc. to run a different scenario, just know that everything is at a risk that you're seeing that has that color."

>> Meg Halford: "The one on the bottom is expected risk to structures. This one bugged me big time. But we couldn't change the fuel model. If you flipped that around, the right side should show very very red, deep red, because of the population and the density, the structures that we have in the east of Boulder County, we know this."

### Fuel Model H: Homes as Fuel

>> Meg Halford: "When the fire enters the urban area, when it transitions from the grasslands or the shrubs or wherever it is not a wildfire, it becomes a wild an urban conflagration. When you have that home to home to home ignition. So I call the homes fuel model H. We actually have a fuel model H in our indices. But that's what I'm hoping that we can work with our modelers is what does that look like when you put that as a fuel model."

## National Cohesive Fire Strategy Framework

>> Meg Halford: "The CWPP really uses the national cohesive fire strategy. That's how CWPPs came about back in 2009. And that's really where we focus the three buckets on the fire adapted communities, your preparedness, your outreach, your education, your structure ignitability, your resilient landscapes, that's that mitigation, and then your safe and effective wildfire response, that's your alert and response."

### Development Process

>> Meg Halford: "How did we create this? We formed a CWPP core team. Several folks in this room were a part of that core team. We had 15 core team executive core team members and then over 100 stakeholders of different fire protection districts, expertise, scientists, fuel modelers that we brought together that we consulted with on the side to help us inform how do we move forward with the CWPP."

>> Meg Halford: "And last and definitely not least was public input. That really informed when we were able to at our community events to really talk to the folks and get their concerns, their challenges, their fears and integrate what we could into the CWPP."

### Fire Adapted Community Model

>> Meg Halford: "Really what we're trying to get down to is that community community mitigation planning. That community local level actions that you can all take. If we go about this at a home here and a home there, it's not going to do us any good. We really have to approach preparedness at a community level."

>> Meg Halford: "That big old model right there is really what we're trying to work and we hope that we can get local level leaders and our communities to work at. We're working together as a collaborative process. We're partners. So, who is doing what? What's your local police department doing and working with your fire protection district? How are you pulling in the county office of disaster management or the sheriff's office? And how are you pulling all those partners together to really create a guiding document for the city of Louisville."

## Louisville-Specific Actions & Progress

### Completed Work

And I know that they have plans to expand that to cover the entire city and the communities within. And hopefully as a county we can help we can support that. We can help advise and hopefully we get other folks to the table, not just the fire protection district or the city leaders, but you're also bringing in all those important players that I listed all the land managers, the police department, the office of disaster management that can help you all focus down to this local level planning."

One of them is wildfire mitigation but also wildlife grasslands health recreation aesthetics.
