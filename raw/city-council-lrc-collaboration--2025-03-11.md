# Louisville City Council Special Meeting: LRC Collaboration Discussion
March 11, 2025

## Meeting Purpose

>> Vanessa Sate, Economic Vitality Manager: "When we talked to city council as well as the LRC, the desire tonight was to be more of a collaborative conversation between the LRC and city council. In your packet, we shared the 2025 2026 LRC work plan that was adopted in December of last year by the LRC. We have taken the feedback to do a two-year work plan for the LRC because a lot of their projects are real estate development and longer term. I just felt it showed a better runway what the LRC was working on to do it every two years instead of every one year."

>> Vanessa Sate: "In preparation for this meeting as well, we talked to the LRC on where there might potentially be pain points in collaboration and cooperation between the LRC and city council. We also wanted to use this time to have that conversation on what everyone would like to see and how everyone can work together."

## Mayor's Framing

>> Mayor: "There is a shared purpose and vision between these two groups and they have different roles and they have different jobs and different ways of becoming part of their respective boards. But we're all here to do the same thing, which is to help the city of Louisville, specifically in this part of town. And there are moments in which the relationship are awkward. You know, passing things back and forth or you know, who gets final approval of what or where is the direction coming from or how much autonomy is needed, all those things that can happen."

>> Mayor: "The LRC is so special in its having its own funding mechanism and so it's different than any other board we've worked with. I think there's a lot of excitement about looking at how we currently operate with each other and if there's slight tweaks we can look at. And as Cliff said in our last meeting, not an expectation tonight that we're going to have pen to paper on what those changes are, but more like what direction do we see these two entities going."

>> Mayor: "As most of us know the funding mechanism runs out in 2032, but the organization needs to continue on past that in perpetuity. So, this is a long-term relationship and we have really big things going on with front and center and the underpass and other aspects over these next few years and just trying to understand how we work together and what our roles are."

>> Mayor: "Our attorney Cory's been really helpful in also kind of laying out the good and bad about the current situation. We can play by those rules or we can adjust those rules. We all have the power to do that. Generally speaking, the council empowers the LRC to do a specific task and then the LRC's job is to go ahead and carry that out. And we have put a lot of maybe extra burdens on our LRC that some other communities don't put on their LRCs or different oversight. That might be how we want to continue it. We might want to continue as we are or we might want to look at some of that oversight."

>> Mayor: "I like comparing it to the facade improvement program. They have permission within that facade approval program to kind of do what they see fit under that program because they ask permission to do that thing. Do they have permission to just kind of do the work that is generally in the language?"

## LRC Perspective on Process Constraints

>> Corey Williams, LRC: "Sitting on the LRC and having to go back and forth multiple times to get approval from city council can just make our process feel a lot more clunky than it feels like it needs to be. The fact that we work so closely with city staff, have an attorney assigned to us, and we have Kila or a city council member on our commission. It feels relatively safe to just give us the permission to move forward and go and do within the parameters of that charter."

>> Corey Williams: "From my perspective, I would love this meeting to serve as an opportunity for us to think about perhaps even getting closer to modifying the IGA so that it's less restrictive based on trust and we have guard rails in place on purpose and just kind of get the sense of where city council is on that. All of our focus is now really on the vision plan and putting the front street and main street vision into play."

## Current Budget Approval Process

>> Vanessa Sate: "Just some background for those that may not be as familiar with the LRC. We as in staff provide city council with a draft budget of the LRC's budget by September of every year. LRC approves their budget, council approves their budget, and then LRC has to approve it again. So that back and forth is common with any policy change or things like that."

>> Vanessa Sate: "The IGA was amended in 2023 to allow the LRC to approve a grant if it's already within their budgeted amount. So, if they have a budgeted amount for like their facade improvement programming and their property improvement programming, if they approve a grant that's already aligned with that budget, then they just approve it. But if they were to go out of budget or to make any budget amendment, again, it starts city council, then the LRC, then city council again."

## Legal Context: Urban Renewal Authority Structure

>> Corey Hoffman, LRC Attorney: "I will admit I'm new to the party. So, if there's history that I don't know about, but the statute for urban renewal contemplates that the job of the urban renewal authority is to implement the plan of council. And when council selects a body to be a separate urban renewal authority as opposed to council acting as the authority, I think the goal is to create what I would call subject matter experts."

>> Corey Hoffman: "The idea is that a plan, the urban renewal plan is the document that council or that the urban renewal authority implements on behalf of the council. So, it's supposed to be doing council's work and at the same time perhaps if empowered maybe advising council on where new urban renewal authorities or areas could be created because the clock in an urban renewal authority the TIF is collected for 25 years but the area lasts forever and it's intended to be the blight is an ongoing attempt to remediate and to look at new areas to prevent the spread of blight."

>> Corey Hoffman: "The agreement that's in place, there's two components that I would say are if you were to look at the industry, if you will, and I represent about 15 urban renewal authorities across the state, there are provisions in the IGA that are unique."

### Unique Budget Process

>> Corey Hoffman: "The fact of taking of getting budgetary approval from council is unique. The urban renewal authority is a separate governmental entity under Colorado law. It is subject to the local government budget act. And so the IGA puts an additional step on even the adoption of the budget."

### Unique Redevelopment Agreement Process

>> Corey Hoffman: "What Corey and Lexi mentioned about redevelopment agreements, the way the IGA is drafted, unless something is explicitly in the LRC budget, which has already been approved by council, it has to first be approved by the LRC, then be approved by council, then come back to the LRC for final approval. That's a three-month process in some cases because the LRC meets once a month. Highly unusual because the LRC has statutory authority to enter into agreements that are consistent with the urban renewal plan."

>> Corey Hoffman: "The urban renewal plan is the policy document that council adopts that says to the LRC, go forth and do your work. And so big picture it is supposed to be a collaborative relationship. There is a term that has been used about what if an urban renewal authority goes rogue. And in 25 or 30 years I've seen it happen in a couple of communities. But there are mechanisms in place for council if that were to happen because ultimately the job of the urban renewal authority is to implement council's policy."

>> Corey Hoffman: "If the urban renewal authority isn't doing that, you have a much more fundamental structural problem in my view. I think what the Urban Renewal Authority talked about, what the LRC talked about was this idea of the current IGA is more of an oversight document than it is a collaboration document. And so, if council was so inclined, I think the LRC certainly has suggestions on how the IGA could be streamlined that would make it more collaborative."

>> Corey Hoffman: "If one of the goals of council and the LRC and this is a policy goal that is obviously up to the two bodies is to be more efficient and responsive to the business community within the urban renewal authority area, taking some of those steps out so long as it's still consistent with council's direction would make the process more efficient."

## Specific IGA Constraints

>> Corey Hoffman: "I would say there's two other pieces in the IGA. One is any proposed expenditure by the LRC which has not been previously approved as part of the LRC budget shall be subject to prior review and approval by council."

>> Vanessa Sate: "For example, we have to take a budget amendment forward to the LRC in April and then it will be taking our budget amendment forward in May with the rest of the city's budget amendment and then it has to go back to the LRC for final budget approval."

>> Corey Hoffman: "And then redevelopment agreements in general. Section 5D of the current agreement says essentially any redevelopment agreement has to go to council in addition to the LRC. And oftentimes if for example there is a new proposed development that wants to seek a reimbursement from newly generated property tax increment. That is a very typical type of urban renewal undertaking that would not in most cases require council approval, but in Louisville it does. And you're talking about net new revenue in those types of agreements where TIF would be generated from the redevelopment of a particular property or properties."

>> Corey Hoffman: "It is wonky to have to have that three-step process to do what is a fairly typical type of agreement and the type of agreement that really doesn't put anyone at risk. The only one at risk is the property owner because if TIF isn't generated from the redevelopment of the particular property, there's no money to be shared. So from a risk assessment perspective, there's very little risk."

>> Corey Hoffman: "What is probably most unique about the LRC that folks don't realize because it doesn't impose a tax, the LRC is not subject to TABOR. That certainly makes it more efficient. But it could be the origin of some of the concern. But you know there is language in the IGA about bonds. I would think that that is the type of collaboration the city absolutely should be engaged in. So I'm not suggesting that is something that would be refined. But other agreements where there's no pledge of revenue other than net new revenue that is at least should be consistent with the council's plan, i.e. the urban renewal plan. Why not make it more streamlined?"

## Staff Review Process

>> Vanessa Sate: "Staff reviews all the applications that go in to any of the LRC programs the same way we review facade applications that get approved by the city. And so we are vetting to a certain extent these applications before they come before the LRC to make sure that they're aligned with programs to make sure they're aligned with the blight mitigation factors as well as that there's something to be able to give back to them. They're generating something."
